Sample lesson plans

Lessons built around meaningful language use

These three anonymized samples move from everyday directions and restaurant conversations to academic graph presentations. Each plan includes objectives, key language, materials, a sequence, and an assessment approach.

  1. Getting Around Town: Asking for Directions

    Learner level: A1–A2 · Approximately 60–65 minutes

    Ask for directions and locate familiar places, including a restroom. Ask and answer questions using simple present forms and practice at least five direction words.

    Download the complete anonymized lesson plan as a Word document

    View lesson details on this page

    Objectives

    • Ask for directions and locate familiar places, including a restroom.
    • Ask and answer questions using simple present forms and practice at least five direction words.

    Key language

    • left, right, straight, turn, go back, stop, follow
    • Where is the ___? Turn left. Go straight. Stop at the ___.

    Materials

    • A simple town map, visual slides or board drawings, paper, and pencils. Colored pencils are optional.

    Lesson sequence

    • Warm up with a question about how learners travel to class and briefly review location phrases.
    • Introduce direction vocabulary with gestures and visual examples; model a short exchange.
    • Have learners follow teacher-given directions, then take turns giving directions.
    • Use a projected or printed map for whole-class practice, then repeat the map task with a partner.
    • Partners role-play a visitor and a local. The visitor asks where a place is; the local gives directions using the map.
    • For an individual check, learners draw a map from spoken directions, compare maps, and revise their work.
    • Close with a 3–2–1 exit response: three new words, two phrases used, and one question.

    Assessment and scaffolding

    • Listen for clear direction words during partner role-play. Review each learner’s map and exit response. Model one exchange first, use visual cues, and allow rehearsal with a partner before independent work.

  2. Explaining Charts and Graphs in Presentations

    Learner level: B1–B2 · Approximately 2 hours, including breaks; the source plan includes a two-hour sequence

    Identify and label five graph types, select a suitable graph for a question, and explain trends in data. Use simple present forms, graph vocabulary, and descriptive adjectives to talk about data.

    Download the complete anonymized lesson plan as a Word document

    View lesson details on this page

    Objectives

    • Identify and label five graph types, select a suitable graph for a question, and explain trends in data.
    • Use simple present forms, graph vocabulary, and descriptive adjectives to talk about data.

    Key language

    • independent variable, dependent variable, x-axis, y-axis, pie chart, bar graph, box-and-whisker plot, scatterplot, line graph
    • This graph shows the effect of ___ on ___. As you can see here, ___. This is ___ because ___.

    Materials

    • Board or display, paper, markers, short graph titles, sample charts, and a survey tool or equivalent paper survey.

    Lesson sequence

    • Review presentation parts and descriptive adjectives, then explain the lesson objectives.
    • Model graph vocabulary by drawing and labeling examples. Learners copy and name the graphs.
    • As a class, match sample titles to graph types and explain why each graph fits.
    • Partners create titles, choose graph types, and share selected examples with the group.
    • Use a matching task to connect titles and graph forms. Model a short presentation of a graph, then guide the class through another example.
    • Partners practice explaining sample graphs using sentence frames and take turns as speaker and listener.
    • Pairs conduct a short class survey, organize results in a graph, and prepare an oral explanation.
    • Learners explain one graph type and when to use it, then review the objectives and prepare a group presentation if time allows.

    Assessment and scaffolding

    • Assess graph selection and explanations during guided practice, partner presentations, and the survey task. Provide labeled models, sentence frames, and structured partner turns before asking learners to present independently.

  3. Ordering Food at a Restaurant

    Learner level: A1–A2 · Extended lesson; allow approximately 90 minutes for the full sequence and optional activities

    Use at least three phrases in a short restaurant conversation, ask and answer about prices, read a basic menu, and calculate a tip. Practice simple present questions and learn five or more food-related words.

    Download the complete anonymized lesson plan as a Word document

    View lesson details on this page

    Objectives

    • Use at least three phrases in a short restaurant conversation, ask and answer about prices, read a basic menu, and calculate a tip.
    • Practice simple present questions and learn five or more food-related words.

    Key language

    • menu, order, with, without, sweet, salty, bland, spicy, bitter, sour
    • Can I get ___? I’ll take the ___. Can I get it to go? How much is ___?

    Materials

    • Picture prompts or food items, sample menus with prices, flashcards, paper and markers, and optional digital vocabulary practice.

    Lesson sequence

    • Begin with food pictures and invite learners to name dishes or identify ingredients. Review direction language from the previous lesson.
    • Introduce food and taste vocabulary with pictures, objects, and clear examples.
    • Use a quick passing game for learners to practice ordering a pictured item. Invite learners to describe foods with adjectives.
    • Model how a menu is organized. Learners locate items and prices, first together and then with a partner.
    • Partners practice ordering from a menu. Add a short listening example, then repeat a customer-and-server role-play.
    • Discuss tipping as part of the sample restaurant interaction. Learners create a simple restaurant menu and take turns being the customer and server.
    • Close by revisiting the objectives and inviting questions. Optional extensions include food bingo or a telephone message game.

    Assessment and scaffolding

    • Listen for understandable orders, price questions, and responses during the role-play. Check whether learners can find items and prices on a menu. Model each exchange, use picture prompts and sentence frames, and let pairs rehearse before the restaurant simulation.
